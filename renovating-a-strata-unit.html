Renovating a strata unit in BC: approvals, bylaws and common mistakes

Which renovations need strata approval, alteration agreements, flooring and noise bylaws, permits, and how to avoid disputes with neighbours.

Renovating a condo or townhouse is not like renovating a house. Walls, pipes and floors are often common property or affect your neighbours, and strata bylaws decide what you can change, when you can work and who pays if something goes wrong.

What usually needs approval

  • Changes to common property or limited common property (balconies, patios, exterior doors, windows).
  • Anything touching structure, plumbing stacks, gas lines or the building's electrical system.
  • Flooring changes, especially replacing carpet with hard surfaces, because of noise transfer.
  • Moving kitchens or bathrooms, which means moving drains.

Purely cosmetic work inside your unit (paint, new cabinet doors, light fixtures) usually needs no approval, but check your bylaws.

Alteration agreements

For approved alterations, stratas commonly require an alteration and indemnity agreement. You agree to pay for the work, maintain it, carry insurance, and be responsible for damage it causes, now and for future owners. It runs with the unit, so buyers inherit the obligation. When buying, ask for any alteration agreements registered against the unit.

A sample budget

The total here is $61,500. Add 10% to 15% for surprises: older buildings often hide aluminium wiring, poly-B piping or asbestos in drywall compound and flooring.

Flooring and noise

Many bylaws require an acoustic underlay meeting a minimum sound rating (often stated as an IIC or delta-IIC value) when installing hard flooring. Ignoring this is one of the most common causes of Civil Resolution Tribunal claims. Get the approved product in writing before you order.

Working hours and logistics

Bylaws set renovation hours, elevator booking, padding requirements, and where contractors can park and dump materials. Some charge move-in or damage deposits. Tell neighbours before noisy work starts; it prevents more disputes than any bylaw.

Permits

Municipal permits are still required for structural, plumbing and electrical work. Unpermitted work can cause insurance problems and must be disclosed when selling. Older condos with original 1970s to 1990s finishes are where most renovation value lies; current prices for renovated versus original units are easy to compare on EstateBlock.

Questions people ask

Do I need strata approval to replace carpet with laminate?

Usually yes. Most bylaws regulate hard flooring and require acoustic underlay.

Who pays if my renovation causes a leak downstairs?

Usually you, under the bylaws and any alteration agreement, plus the insurance deductible if a claim is made.

Can the strata refuse my renovation?

It can refuse changes to common property and enforce bylaws, but must act reasonably and consistently.

Does an alteration agreement transfer to a buyer?

Typically yes; it runs with the unit.
